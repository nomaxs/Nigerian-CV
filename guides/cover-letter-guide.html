How to Write a Nigerian Cover Letter

A cover letter is your one chance to explain, in your own words, why you specifically are worth interviewing. Here's the structure that works.

On this page

1. The letter structure 2. Who to address it to 3. What each paragraph should do 4. Getting the tone right 5. Common mistakes

1. The letter structure

A Nigerian cover letter follows a formal business-letter format, whether it's attached as a PDF or pasted into an email body:

  1. Your details — name, phone, email and location at the top.
  2. The date.
  3. The recipient's details — hiring manager's name and title if you have it, company name, and address.
  4. Salutation — see below for how to choose this correctly.
  5. Three short paragraphs — who you are and the role you want, why you're a strong fit with evidence, and a polite call to action.
  6. Sign-off — "Yours faithfully," if you don't know the recipient's name, "Yours sincerely," if you do, followed by your name.

2. Who to address it to

Always try to find an actual name — check the job advert, the company website, or LinkedIn. "Dear Mr. Adeyemi," reads far better than a generic greeting and shows you did your homework. If you genuinely can't find one:

  • Use "Dear Hiring Manager," for corporate and private-sector roles.
  • Use "Dear Sir/Madam," for government, education, and more traditional/formal organisations — this is still the expected default in many public-sector applications in Nigeria.

3. What each paragraph should do

Paragraph 1 — the opening

State the exact role you're applying for and where you saw it advertised, then one sentence on your headline qualification. Don't waste the opening line restating your CV — get straight to why you're writing.

Paragraph 2 — the evidence

This is the paragraph that does the real work. Pick your single strongest, most relevant achievement — ideally with a number — and connect it directly to what the job needs. If the advert asks for someone who can "manage vendor relationships," and you've done exactly that, say so explicitly rather than hoping the connection is obvious.

Paragraph 3 — the close

A short, confident closing line inviting an interview: "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my experience can support your team." Avoid sounding desperate ("I really need this job") or overly casual ("Let's chat!") — a Nigerian cover letter stays formal even when the industry is informal.

4. Getting the tone right

Formal, respectful, and specific — that's the register most Nigerian employers expect, across sectors from banking to tech. That doesn't mean stiff or robotic: write in full, natural sentences, avoid overused stock phrases ("I am a hardworking and dedicated individual" says nothing a recruiter hasn't read a thousand times), and let your actual experience do the persuading.

Every job category on this site has its own guide with a tailored opening line and evidence points for that specific role — see the full guide index.

5. Common mistakes

  • Repeating your CV word for word. The cover letter should add context and personality your CV's bullet points can't — not summarise it.
  • Generic letters that could go to any company. If you can swap out the company name and it still makes sense, it's too generic. Mention something specific about the employer where you can.
  • Getting the salutation-to-closing pairing wrong. "Dear Sir/Madam" should close with "Yours faithfully," — pairing it with "Yours sincerely," (reserved for when you've named the recipient) is a small but noticeable slip.
  • Making it too long. One page, three to four short paragraphs. A cover letter is a door-opener, not a second CV.
