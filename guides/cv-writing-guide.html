How to Write a Nigerian CV That Gets Interviews

A practical, no-fluff walkthrough of what belongs in a Nigerian CV, in what order, and the small details that make recruiters take you seriously.

On this page

1. The right structure 2. What goes in each section 3. Listing WAEC, NYSC and your degree correctly 4. Common mistakes that cost interviews 5. Tailoring your CV to the job 6. Length, format and file type

1. The right structure

Most Nigerian employers — from banks to schools to oil and gas companies — expect a CV in a familiar order. Stray from it and a recruiter has to hunt for information, which is the fastest way to get skipped over in a stack of 200 applications. Use this order unless a job advert asks for something specific:

  1. Full name and title — your name in large text, with the role you're applying for or your professional title just beneath it.
  2. Contact details — phone number, email address, and city/state. You don't need your full house address.
  3. Professional summary — 2–3 sentences on who you are and what you bring. This has replaced the older "Objectives" section on most modern CVs.
  4. Work experience — most recent role first, with achievements, not just duties.
  5. Education — degree first, then O'Level (WAEC/NECO) beneath it.
  6. Skills, certifications and languages — keep these scannable, not paragraphs.
  7. References — two people who can vouch for you, or "Available on request" if space is tight.

2. What goes in each section

Professional summary

Two to three sentences, written in the third person or without pronouns at all. Say how many years of experience you have, your core strength, and one number that proves it. For example: "Customer service representative with 3 years' experience resolving over 70 inquiries daily across phone and chat, with a satisfaction score consistently above 92%." A number beats an adjective every time.

Work experience

For each role, list the company, your title, and the dates (month and year is fine — you don't need exact days). Underneath, write 2–4 bullet points that lead with an action verb and, wherever possible, a result: "Reduced complaint resolution time by 30% by introducing a new escalation process," not "Responsible for handling complaints." Recruiters skim bullet points; make the first three words of each one count.

Tip: If you're currently serving with NYSC or just finished, your Place of Primary Assignment (PPA) counts as real work experience. List it like any other job — employer name, your role, dates, and what you actually did.

3. Listing WAEC, NYSC and your degree correctly

Nigerian CVs have a few conventions that differ from CVs elsewhere, and getting them right signals attention to detail:

  • Degree first, WAEC/NECO beneath it — once you have a degree or HND, your secondary school result becomes a supporting line, not the headline. List the institution, course, class of degree (e.g. Second Class Upper), and years attended.
  • WAEC/NECO format — write it as: "WAEC/SSCE — 6 Credits including Mathematics and English, 2019." You don't need to list every subject and grade.
  • NYSC — if you've completed service, you can note "NYSC Completed" near your contact details or education section. If you're currently serving, mention it in your title/summary, since many entry-level roles specifically want corps members.
  • Professional certifications (ICAN, CIBN, COREN, PMP, TRCN, NMCN, and so on) deserve their own section — they're often the deciding factor between two similar candidates.

4. Common mistakes that cost interviews

  • Using an old-style "BIO-DATA" block (date of birth, marital status, state of origin, religion) at the top. Most employers no longer expect this, it takes up prime space, and in some sectors it invites unconscious bias. Leave it off unless a specific employer's application form asks for it.
  • Listing duties instead of achievements. "Managed a team of 5" tells a recruiter what your job was. "Managed a team of 5, hitting 105% of quarterly targets" tells them you were good at it.
  • An unprofessional email address. Create a simple firstname.lastname@gmail.com if you don't already have one — it takes five minutes.
  • No phone number that works. Double check it, and make sure your voicemail (if any) is something you'd want a recruiter to hear.
  • Sending the same CV to every job. See the tailoring section below — this is the single biggest lever you have.

5. Tailoring your CV to the job

You don't need a completely different CV for every application, but you should adjust three things each time: your professional title/summary (mirror the job title from the advert), the order of your skills (put the most relevant ones first), and which achievements you lead with. A CV that echoes the language of the job advert — without copying it word for word — reads as a stronger fit, both to a human recruiter and to any applicant-tracking software screening CVs before a human sees them.

6. Length, format and file type

One page if you have under 5 years of experience, two pages maximum otherwise — Nigerian recruiters rarely read past page two on a first pass. Always send a CV as a PDF, never as an editable Word document, so your formatting doesn't break on someone else's computer. Name the file sensibly: Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf, not CV(3)final_use this one.pdf.
